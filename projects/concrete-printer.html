Motion Control · R&D · Systems Integration

Cable-Driven Parallel 3D Concrete Printer

A large-scale robotic printing platform built around movable cable towers, a suspended cubic end effector and multidisciplinary control/electronics integration.

Motion ControlPIDOpenCVHMIElectronics

System architecture

Four movable tower stations supported a cable-suspended cubic end effector carrying the extrusion nozzle. Screw jacks provided a rigid operating base after positioning.

My contribution

Electronics, control integration, wiring, motion-system bring-up, HMI work, testing, troubleshooting and multidisciplinary engineering support.

Control concept

Motion Controller→Motor / Cable Axes→ Suspended End Effector↔Camera Feedback→PID Correction

The challenge

Coordinating a large physical system with multiple interacting subsystems

The project required motion, electronics, mechanics, software and calibration to work as one platform rather than as isolated components.

Mechanical & motion architecture

Movable towersTower stations could be repositioned for setup flexibility and then stabilized for operation using lowered screw jacks.
Suspended end effectorA cubic cable-driven end effector carried the extrusion nozzle and moved in XYZ through coordinated cable motion.

Controls & electronics

Motion controlThe platform incorporated multi-axis industrial motion hardware, motor/drive integration and closed-loop correction work.
Human interfaceHMI functions supported setup and tower-related adjustment/operation during system development.

Camera-assisted correction

ObservationAn overhead camera was used as an additional measurement source for positional error.
Correction conceptOpenCV-based positional feedback supported PID-style correction of observed error during R&D.

Validation

From integration to physical printing

The platform progressed beyond bench integration to actual concrete-printing trials, providing a full-system test of motion, electronics, software and extrusion behavior.

A collaborator’s public project article reports a 14-month development effort and approximately 2–2.5 mm printing accuracy; those figures are attributed to that article rather than presented here as my independent measurement.
